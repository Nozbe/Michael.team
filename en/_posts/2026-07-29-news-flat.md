# Sub-projects suck (and I have the research to prove it)

For 19 years I've been refusing to add sub-projects and sub-tasks to Nozbe. Customers left us over it. Many came back. Turns out four decades of research quietly agree with me…

## 🌳 The feature everybody asks for

*"I'd switch to you tomorrow, but you don't have sub-projects. C'mon Michael, it's an obvious feature you're missing!"* — I've heard this hundreds of times. The answer has always been NO. Not because it's hard to build, but because I've watched what happens to people who get it.

## 🧾 A hierarchy is a decision machine

Every time you add a task: *"where does this belong?"* Every time you look for it: *"where did I put it?"* Building the structure feels like progress — it isn't. It's a tax you pay forever.

## 🐢 Deeper means slower and more wrong

Computer scientists have been testing *broad and shallow* versus *narrow and deep* since the early eighties. Kiger (1984) gave people the same 64 items arranged five ways: time and errors both went up as the structure got deeper.

## ⏱️ And it breaks exactly when you're busy

Wallace, Anderson and Shneiderman (1987) put users under time pressure. The ones on the deep tree made **96% more errors** and took **16% longer**. Which is, let's be honest, when you actually use your to-do app.

## 📁 What people do when nobody's watching

Bergman and colleagues (2010) had 296 people retrieve their own real files from their own real computers. Average depth: **2.86 folders**. Given total freedom, humans quietly go flat.

## 🧠 Your head holds four things — not four levels

Miller said seven plus or minus two. Cowan (2001) revised it down to about **four chunks**. Every level you traverse eats some of that budget — on looking for the work instead of getting it done.

## 🪂 Scrolling is like falling

Social media apps know this. A flat list asks nothing of your memory — gravity does the work. Digging through projects → sub-projects → tasks → sub-tasks feels like climbing.

## ✅ But breaking things down DOES help

Kruger and Evans (2004): listing the steps cuts the planning fallacy by more than half. And the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist (NEJM, 2009) cut in-hospital deaths from 1.5% to 0.8% — with **19 items on one piece of paper**. Not a tree. A *flat* list.

**Decomposition is good, hierarchy is not.** That's why [Nozbe](https://nozbe.com/?c=newsletter) solves "sub-tasks" with checklists inside comments — and when a checklist outgrows itself, one click turns it into a project.

## 🗂️ What we give you instead

**Project groups** (one project can live in *several* groups — a tree makes you pick one) and **project sections** (a vertical Kanban you see in one scroll). Organization without depth.
